How to Negotiate a Raise With Data: A Step-by-Step Playbook

By the 55300 Editorial TeamUpdated October 21, 20268 min read

A practical, numbers-based process for asking for more pay — research, timing, the exact ask and how to handle 'no'.

Step 1: Know your number

Gather three data points: market pay for your role and location (public salary surveys, job postings that list pay ranges), inflation since your last raise, and the measurable value you created. Turn each into dollars. A raise request built on numbers is a business decision for your manager; one built on need is a favor.

Step 2: Choose the timing

The best windows are 4–8 weeks before budgets are set, right after a visible win, or when your responsibilities have clearly grown. The worst are during layoffs or the week of a missed target.

Step 3: Anchor with a specific figure

Ask for a specific number slightly above your target — '$61,500' rather than 'around $60k'. Precise numbers signal research. Anchoring research in negotiation consistently shows that the first credible number shapes the final outcome.

Script: "Over the last year I took over X and delivered Y, worth about $Z. Market data for this role in our area is $A–$B. I'd like to move my base to $C. What would it take to get there this cycle?"

Step 4: Translate the ask

Show your manager what it costs. A move from $55,300 to $59,000 is $3,700 a year — about $308 a month, or less than 7% of base. Use the raise calculator to show the after-tax value to you and keep emotion out of it.

Step 5: Handle 'not now'

  • Ask what specific results would justify the raise and by when — get it in writing.
  • Negotiate non-salary items: title, remote days, training budget, extra PTO, an earlier review date.
  • Set a calendar check-in. If the goalposts move twice, the market is your answer.

Step 6: The outside offer

Changing employers often produces a larger pay increase than staying, because new hires are priced at market. If you use an outside offer as leverage, be prepared to accept it.
